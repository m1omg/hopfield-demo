Associative memory · 1982

A network that remembers by rolling downhill.

A Hopfield network stores patterns as the minima of an energy function. Show it a corrupted, half-erased, or barely recognisable fragment and it does only one thing — repeatedly flip whichever neuron disagrees with its neighbours — until the energy can fall no further. Wherever it stops is the recalled memory. No gradients, no training loop, no backpropagation. Everything on this page runs live in your browser.

02 What the network actually is

There is no architecture to speak of — just one symmetric matrix. Here are the 65,536 weights that hold every memory you stored, and, beside them, how similar those memories are to one another — which is exactly what decides whether recall works.

03 The energy landscape

State space has 2256 corners, so any picture of it is a slice. This is the slice physicists draw: the plane of overlaps with two chosen memories. Every point is a real state whose true energy is evaluated with the full weight matrix — dark is low, bright is high. The four vertices of the diamond are the two memories and their mirror images, each at the bottom of its own valley. 04 How much can it hold?

Push past a critical load and a Hopfield network does not degrade gracefully — it collapses. This runs a real Monte-Carlo experiment in a background thread: store P random patterns in an N-neuron network, corrupt one, settle, and measure whether the original came back. The Hebbian curve should fall off a cliff right around α = P/N ≈ 0.138.

05 The whole model, on one page

The classical Hopfield network is small enough to state completely. Here it is.

1 · State

N neurons, each either firing or not:

s ∈ {−1, +1}N

Here N = 256, laid out as a 16×16 image purely so you can see it. The network has no idea it is looking at a picture.

2 · Storage

Hebb's rule — neurons that fire together wire together:

wij = 1N Σμ=1P ξiμ ξjμ, wii = 0

One pass over the data, no iteration, and the matrix is symmetric — which is the whole reason the dynamics below are guaranteed to stop.

3 · Dynamics

Pick a neuron, look at what everyone else is telling it, agree with the majority:

si ← sgn( Σj wij sj )

Update one neuron at a time (asynchronously) and the network always converges. Update them all at once and it can fall into a two-cycle instead — try it in the lab.

4 · Energy

Every update is a step downhill on the Lyapunov function

E(s) = −12 Σi≠j wij si sj

Flipping neuron i changes the energy by ΔE = 2 si hi, and a flip only happens when that quantity is negative. E can only decrease, it is bounded below, and there are finitely many states — so the network must halt. That three-line argument is the entire convergence proof.

5 · Capacity

Crosstalk between memories grows as you add them. The signal-to-noise analysis gives a sharp phase transition at

αc = P / N ≈ 0.138

for Hebbian storage — about 35 memories in this 256-neuron network. Amit, Gutfreund and Sompolinsky derived it with the replica method in 1985; section 04 measures it.

6 · What else lives down there

The valleys you asked for are not the only ones. Every memory's mirror image −ξμ is a minimum too (the energy is even), and odd mixtures such as sgn(ξ1+ξ2+ξ3) create spurious attractors that belong to nothing you stored. The Mixture button drops the probe into one. Raise the temperature and the network can shake itself out again.

The 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Hopfield and Hinton for this line of work. Modern “dense” / continuous Hopfield networks push capacity to exponential in N and turn out to be equivalent to transformer attention — but the model on this page is the 1982 original, implemented exactly as written.
